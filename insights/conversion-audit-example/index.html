What a website conversion audit actually looks for

A conversion audit is not a promise that changing one headline will magically create sales. It is a disciplined way to find places where a website makes visitors work too hard to understand the offer, trust the business, or take the next step.

1. Can a stranger understand the page in five seconds?

The first test is brutally simple: who is this for, what do they get, and what should they do next? If those answers require scrolling, decoding jargon, or reading an origin story, the homepage is spending attention before it has earned it.

Before: “Innovative solutions for a better tomorrow.”

This could describe almost any company. It names neither customer nor outcome.

After: “Book reliable after-hours bookkeeping for your growing small business.”

The rewrite is not automatically perfect, but it reduces ambiguity: audience, service, and intended action are visible immediately.

2. Does the call-to-action match visitor readiness?

A homepage can have too many buttons or one button that asks for too much commitment. We look for a clear primary action and a lower-friction secondary path when the buyer still needs evidence.

  • Primary CTA: the commercially valuable next step.
  • Secondary CTA: proof, sample, pricing context, FAQ, or another confidence-building step.
  • Repeated CTA: available at natural decision points rather than every few lines.

3. Is trust arriving before the ask?

Testimonials, recognizable work, guarantees, process transparency, contact information, and useful specifics all reduce perceived risk. The right proof depends on the business. A local service company and a software tool should not use the same trust architecture.

4. Where is friction hiding?

Friction is not only slow loading or broken forms. It includes vague pricing, inconsistent terminology, unclear service boundaries, competing navigation choices, unreadable mobile sections, weak form labels, or a checkout step that suddenly introduces uncertainty.

5. What gets fixed first?

The useful output is not a fifty-item criticism list. It is a sequence. We prioritize changes by how close they sit to the conversion path and how cheaply they can be tested.

Example 7-day sequence
  1. Rewrite the hero around customer + outcome + action.
  2. Choose one primary CTA and make its label specific.
  3. Move strongest proof beside the first major ask.
  4. Remove one unnecessary decision from the navigation.
  5. Clarify what happens after the form is submitted.
  6. Check mobile hierarchy and form usability.
  7. Measure results before adding more changes.
